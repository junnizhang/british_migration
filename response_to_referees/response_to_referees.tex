\section*{Referee 1}
\textbf{Recommendation: Major Revision}

\textbf{Overall assessment}

This manuscript addresses an important applied problem in demographic estimation and forecasting. The highly disaggregated UK internal-migration data present genuine challenges arising from heterogeneity across age, sex, origin, destination, and time, and the authors construct a flexible Bayesian hierarchical framework that is capable of borrowing information across a very large number of cells. The manuscript is generally clearly written, the progression from the baseline specification to richer interaction structures is easy to follow, and the reported computational feasibility of fitting a large hierarchical model is potentially useful for applied demographic work. The empirical examples also illustrate why flexible age and origin--destination structure can matter in practice. At the same time, I believe substantial revision is needed before the contribution of the paper can be properly assessed. In particular, the current manuscript does not yet distinguish clearly enough between methodological development and the application/integration of standard Bayesian hierarchical components; the regularization mechanism in the very high-dimensional specification needs a more explicit treatment; the inferential computation requires a clearer description; and the forecasting claims need to be supported by genuine out-of-sample validation. I therefore recommend major revision. My main comments are below.
\begin{response}
Thank you for your careful reading and constructive suggestions. We have carefully addressed all of your comments and made substantial revisions accordingly.
\end{response}

\section*{Referee 1: Major comments}
\refcomment{Major comment 1}{
The methodological contribution and the gap in the existing literature need to be stated much more precisely. The manuscript repeatedly states that it ``develops several Bayesian models.'' However, as currently presented, the three models appear to be nested specifications within one common hierarchical framework rather than substantially distinct Bayesian models. The baseline model combines a Poisson--Gamma hierarchy with standard Gaussian hierarchical effects, random-walk priors, an AR(2) temporal component, and interaction terms; the two expanded specifications then add higher-order interaction terms. I encourage the authors to clarify exactly what is new. It would be helpful to distinguish explicitly among: (i) a new statistical model or prior construction, (ii) a new computational strategy, and (iii) an application-specific integration of established Bayesian hierarchical components for highly disaggregated migration data. If the principal contribution is the construction of a sufficiently flexible and computationally feasible modeling framework for this application, that is a legitimate contribution, but it should be stated directly and the methodological claims should be calibrated accordingly. Relatedly, the introduction currently establishes mainly that the Bayesian literature on internal migration is limited. This is not yet a sufficiently specific methodological gap. The revised manuscript should compare the proposed framework directly with the closest existing approaches and explain which limitation of previous methods motivates each important modeling component. For example, are existing methods unable to accommodate single-year age profiles, origin--destination-specific heterogeneity, changing age profiles over time, very high-order interactions, or the required level of computational scalability? A concise comparison table covering dimensions modeled, age smoothing, origin--destination structure, temporal dynamics, uncertainty quantification, and computational strategy would substantially strengthen the motivation.
}
\begin{response}
Thanks for the comment. We have now clarified in the Introduction that the Bayesian models in this paper are applications of a flexible Bayesian hierarchical modeling framework for estimating and forecasting disaggregated demographic rates, developed through more than a decade of work. We have also clarified that we have developed flexible and computationally efficient software for fitting, checking, and using these models.

In the Introduction, we note that we have found only four prior papers in the Bayesian literature on internal migration.  \citet{wisniowski2014baysian} develop a time series model for the logarithm of the total count of out-migration from Scotland to the rest of the UK, international immigration to Scotland, and international emigration from Scotland. \citet{furfaro2021bayesian} develop a hierarchical regression model for migration counts from province 
$i$ to province $j$ at time $t$ in Italy. The migration counts in these two studies are not disaggregated by age or sex. For each region in Bavaria, Germany, and for each sex separately, \citet{goes2026probabilistic} develop a time series model for total net migration counts, use a mixed-effects model to decompose total net migration counts into in-migration and out-migration counts, and use a Dirichlet regression to model the age patterns of in- and out-migration.

By modeling migration rates rather than migration counts, we separate changes in the underlying propensity to migrate from changes in the size and age–sex composition of the population at risk. Furthermore, compared with the separate modeling approach of \citet{goes2026probabilistic}, our joint model allows age profiles of migration to evolve differently over time across regions and sexes while still permitting shared trends across regions or sexes.

\citet{zhang2020bayesian} develop Bayesian hierarchical models within our modeling framework to model internal migration rates in Iceland, also disaggregated by region of origin, region of destination, single year of age, sex, and calendar year. The migration patterns in that setting are less heterogeneous than those considered here, partly because the data period precedes the COVID-19 pandemic. Consequently, the models in \citet{zhang2020bayesian} are less complex than those used in the current paper.

Please see the Introduction for the changes made.
\end{response}

\refcomment{Major comment 2}{
The treatment of high dimensionality, regularization, and shrinkage requires a substantially deeper explanation. The most complex specification contains 121,379 $\beta$ parameters, in addition to 170,352 cell-specific rate quantities. Given that the dataset itself contains 170,352 observed cells, the effective regularization of the model is central to its statistical credibility. Equation (3) gives a useful interpretation of shrinkage of the cell-specific rate $\gamma_{ijast}$ toward its prior mean $\mu_{ijast}$. However, this does not explain how the very large collection of $\beta$ parameters, including the high-order interactions, is regularized. My reading is that the principal mechanism is structured smoothing and partial pooling through Gaussian priors, shared variance parameters, and RW1/RW2 specifications. This should be made explicit. In particular, the current model does not appear to perform sparse shrinkage or interaction selection in the usual sense; instead, it allows all specified interactions to enter while controlling their roughness and magnitude through hierarchical priors. I suggest that the authors provide more evidence on the amount of regularization actually induced by the model. At minimum, posterior summaries of the major variance/smoothing parameters would be informative. Sensitivity to the half-normal hyperpriors would also be useful, especially for the high-order interaction terms. More generally, the authors should explain what prevents the most complex model from becoming a near-saturated smoother and should quantify, where possible, the effective complexity of the higher-order components. This issue is particularly important because the model-comparison procedure favors progressively richer interaction structures.
}
\begin{response}
Thanks for the comment. We have added a new section, ``3.4 Model Identification and Regularization'', to the manuscript. As you point out, we do not perform sparse shrinkage or selection of interactions in the usual sense (e.g., spike-and-slab variable selection in \cite{george1993variable} or horseshoe shrinkage in \cite{carvalho2010horseshoe}). Instead, the roughness and magnitude of the specified interactions are controlled through hierarchical priors. 

We have also added a new section, ``5.2 Estimates of Hyper-parameters'', to the manuscript. Table 5 there shows the point estimates and 95\% credible intervals for the hyper-parameters. Except for $\sigma_{\text{orig:dest}}$, the standard deviation parameters for the interactions are all relatively small, indicating strong regularization. 

We have also added a new section, ``5.3 A Sensitivity Analysis'', to the manuscript. We conduct a sensitivity analysis with respect to the prior scale for the standard deviation parameters. In our main results, all standard deviation parameters have a prior scale of 1. We also considered prior scales of 0.1 and 0.5 and found that the estimates and forecasts of the rates were not sensitive to the choice of prior scale.

Please see the corresponding sections for the changes made.
\end{response}

\refcomment{Major comment 3}{
The computational inference procedure needs a clearer and more complete description. Section 3.3 does not currently provide enough information to understand exactly how posterior inference is performed. The manuscript states that the implementation uses TMB, Laplace approximation, numerical integration, and analytic marginalization of the cell-specific rates, but it later refers to 1,000 posterior draws without explaining how these draws are obtained. Please state clearly which quantities are optimized, which quantities are integrated using the Laplace approximation, and how posterior draws of the parameters, hyperparameters, and derived quantities are generated. If an MCMC step is used at any stage, appropriate convergence and sampling-efficiency diagnostics should be reported (for example, multiple-chain diagnostics, $\widehat{R}$, effective sample sizes, and, where relevant, ESS per second). If the reported posterior draws instead arise from a Gaussian/Laplace approximation, then standard MCMC diagnostics are not applicable; in that case, the authors should explain the approximation explicitly and provide some assessment of its accuracy, ideally through comparison with a more exact posterior computation on one or more reduced-size examples. The reported runtimes are useful, but computational reporting could also be made more informative by specifying the exact processor, number of cores/threads, whether parallelization is used, peak memory consumption, and whether the reported times include generation of the posterior draws. Because computational scalability is emphasized as an advantage of the approach, even a limited empirical assessment of scaling with the number of cells and/or model terms would be valuable. Finally, the description of the analytic integration of $\gamma_{ijast}$ as borrowing from ``collapsed Gibbs sampling'' may be confusing. As presented in the appendix, integrating the Gamma-distributed rates out of the Poisson--Gamma hierarchy yields a Negative-Binomial marginal likelihood directly. If no Gibbs sampler is actually used, the terminology should be revised so that conjugate marginalization is clearly distinguished from a collapsed Gibbs algorithm.}
\begin{response}
Thanks for the comment. We have added more description about computational issues in ``3.5 Computation''. \texttt{TMB} combines a Laplace approximation and numerical integration \citep{kristensen2016tmb,osgood2023statistical}. In the Laplace approximation, we maximize the log marginal posterior density with respect to the $\beta$ parameters, the hyperparameters, and $\xi$. The standard deviation parameters and 
$\xi$ are optimized on the log scale, $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ on the logit scale, and the remaining components on the original scale. We then generate draws of the $\beta$ parameters, the hyperparameters, and $\xi$ 
from a multivariate normal approximation on the transformed scale and back-transform them to the original scale. We obtain draws of $\gamma_{ijast}$ in a post-processing step.

We did not provide a direct comparison between the Laplace approximation and MCMC in our paper. Instead, we point to previous studies. We state in Section 3.5: ``The Laplace approximation underlying \texttt{TMB} belongs to a broader class of approximate Bayesian inference methods that have been shown to be reliable and computationally efficient. \citet{rue2017bayesian} use integrated nested Laplace approximations (INLA) to perform approximate Bayesian inference for latent Gaussian models (LGMs), a class that includes a large proportion of the statistical models used today. They show that INLA (and the associated \texttt{R-INLA} package) performs inference within a reasonable time frame and in most cases is both faster and more accurate than MCMC alternatives. \citet{osgood2023statistical} provide a large-scale simulation study that assesses and compares \texttt{R-INLA} and \texttt{TMB} for continuous spatial models, and find that the predictive fields from both methods are comparable in most situations. We have used our package \texttt{bage} (which builds on \texttt{TMB}) for many demographic estimation and forecasting problems and find the results satisfactory, as assessed by posterior predictive checks and held-out data validation. An example can be found in \citet{bryant2026estimating}.''

Regarding detailed computational reporting, we state in Section 3.5: ``Table 2 shows computation times and memory usage for the initial and expanded models. The computations were performed on a 2023 M2 MacBook Pro. No parallelization was used. The fitting process included taking 2000 draws from the posterior distribution for the $\beta$ parameters, the hyper-parameters, and $\xi$, but did not include post-processing to obtain values for the $\gamma_{ijast}$. Computations for the second extended model took around 7 times longer than those for the initial model. However, at 1531 seconds (26 minutes) these times are still fast for a high-dimensional Bayesian model.''

Regarding the marginalization of $\gamma_{ijast}$, we now note in the new Section ``Model Identification and Regularization'': ``3.4 Note that the Gamma prior for 
$\gamma_{ijast}$ conditional on $\mu_{ijast}$ in (2) is conjugate to the Poisson likelihood for $y_{ijast}$ conditional on $\gamma_{ijast}$ in (1). Consequently, integrating out $\gamma_{ijast}$ yields the negative binomial distribution...'' We also note: ``This is analogous to the marginalization strategy used in collapsed Gibbs sampling \citep{liu1994collapsed}.''

Please see the corresponding sections for the changes made.

\end{response}

\refcomment{Major comment 4}{
Model comparison and, especially, forecasting validation need to be strengthened substantially. Forecasting is central to the title, abstract, and conclusions, yet the manuscript currently does not provide an out-of-sample assessment of forecast performance. WAIC and posterior predictive checks evaluate aspects of fit to the observed 2012--2024 data, but they do not establish that the model forecasts future migration rates accurately. Consequently, the statement that the model ``performs well in forecasting'' is stronger than the empirical evidence currently provided. I strongly recommend a historical forecasting or rolling-origin validation exercise. For example, the authors could fit the model using data ending in earlier years and evaluate one- through five-year-ahead forecasts against subsequently observed data. Appropriate summaries could include RMSE or MAE, predictive log scores, empirical coverage of predictive intervals, interval width, and calibration across forecast horizons. The three proposed specifications should be compared in this exercise, together with at least one simple forecasting benchmark. Such an analysis would also provide a much more direct answer to whether the additional high-order interactions improve forecasting rather than merely in-sample fit. There is also an important issue regarding the current WAIC calculation. The manuscript appears to compute pointwise predictive densities conditional on posterior draws of the observation-specific latent rates $\gamma_{ijast}$, even though these rates are marginalized during fitting and are themselves updated using the corresponding observations. The authors should justify why this conditional WAIC is aligned with the intended predictive target. For prediction of new/future cells, a marginal criterion based on the Negative-Binomial likelihood $p(y_{ijast}\mid\mu_{ijast},\xi)$ may be more relevant. In any case, time-structured out-of-sample validation would be preferable as the primary evidence for forecast performance. In addition, uncertainty in the model-comparison criterion should be reported. The manuscript currently reports only scalar WAIC values. Pointwise differences and a standard error (or an equivalent uncertainty summary) would help readers assess whether the difference between the first and second expanded models is practically meaningful.

}
\begin{response}
Thanks for the comment. We have added a new section, ``4.3 Held-out Data Validation'', to the manuscript. We note: ``The dataset covers the COVID-19 period, and COVID-19 had large and heterogeneous effects on migration. To assess whether our models can capture these effects, we conduct three held-out validation exercises. In the first, we hold out data for 2020–2024, during and after the COVID-19 period, and fit the models using data from 2012–2019, which does not overlap with the COVID-19 period. In the second, we hold out data for 2017–2019, which does not include the COVID-19 period, and fit the models using data from 2012–2016. In the third, we hold out data for 2022–2024 and fit the models using data from 2012–2021, which partially covers the COVID-19 period.''

We also note: ``We calculate several held-out predictive performance measures: the percentage of cells in the held-out data for which the direct estimate of the migration rate, 
$y_{ijast}/P_{ijast}$, falls within the corresponding 50\% prediction interval; the corresponding percentage for the 95\% prediction interval; the median widths of the 50\% and 95\% prediction intervals for held-out cells; and the root mean squared error across held-out cells, computed using the posterior medians of the forecasts as point predictions.''

Four models are compared in the held-out validation exercises: the initial model, the two expanded models, and a main-effects model with no interactions, which serves as a simple baseline for comparison. Details of the main effects model are given in a new Section, ``3.3 Main Effects Model.''

In Section ``4.1 WAIC'', we have revised the WAIC computation to use the Negative-Binomial likelihood $p(y_{ijast}\mid\mu_{ijast},\xi)$. Typically, only scalar WAIC values are reported (e.g., see Section 7.3 of \citet{Gelman2014bayesian}), and we follow this convention.

Please see the corresponding sections for the changes made.
\end{response}
